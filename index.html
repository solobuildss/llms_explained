How Large Language Models Actually Work

From a sequence of typed characters to a trillion parameter neural network producing one word at a time. A complete walkthrough of the math, the machines, and the money behind modern LLMs.

00 / The Short Version

If you only read one thing

The three insights that organize everything else in this guide. Each one is a thread you can pull on for the rest of the article.

PRINCIPLE
It's predict the next token at colossal scale

Every LLM is a stacked Transformer trained to predict the next piece of text. Models in 2026 range from 7 billion to over a trillion parameters, trained on 10 to 15 trillion tokens. The whole apparent intelligence emerges from this one simple objective at extreme scale.

CONSTRAINT
Memory is the real bottleneck, not raw compute

A 70B parameter model in half precision needs about 140 GB of VRAM. That is more than a single H100 (80 GB) can hold. That single number, bytes per parameter times parameter count, explains why GPUs are stacked, networks are exotic, and quantization exists.

ECONOMICS
Training is one time. Inference is forever

Pre-training a frontier model now costs $10M to $100M+ in compute. But once trained, every API call runs the model again. Output token prices have dropped roughly 100x in three years, from about $60 per million tokens at GPT-4 launch to about $0.28 per million for DeepSeek-V3.

01 / Foundations

What is a language model, really?

A Large Language Model is, at its core, a giant differentiable function. It takes a sequence of tokens as input and outputs a probability distribution over what the next token should be. Everything else, the eloquence, the apparent reasoning, the conversational fluency, emerges from doing this one task extremely well at a scale that was unimaginable a decade ago.

The "language model" part is centuries old in spirit: assign probabilities to sequences of words. The "large" part is what changed. When we say a model has 70 billion parameters, we mean it contains 70 billion floating point numbers, the weights, and the model's behavior is determined entirely by their values. Those values are learned by gradient descent on a colossal pile of text.

Parameters as a measure of capacity

Think of each parameter as a knob. The model is a circuit with billions of knobs, and "training" is the process of nudging every knob ever so slightly in the direction that makes the model's predictions better on the training data. For a 70B model, that's 70 billion knobs, all tuned simultaneously.

The empirical magic of LLMs is what's called scaling laws. Model quality improves smoothly and predictably as you scale three things together: parameters, training data, and compute. The famous rough formula for the compute budget is:

Compute budget (FLOPs) C ≈ 6 · N · D

where N is the parameter count and D is the number of training tokens. A 405 billion parameter model trained on 15 trillion tokens needs about 6 × 4.05 × 10¹¹ × 1.5 × 10¹³ ≈ 3.65 × 10²⁵ floating point operations.

A tangible scale

3.65 × 10²⁵ FLOPs is roughly 30 million H100 GPU hours. If you had one H100 working flat out for the entire age of the universe, you would still not finish that training run. You need tens of thousands of GPUs in parallel to make it tractable in months instead of millennia.

A modern AI datacenter rack, the physical substrate of any large language model. Each gold plated card is a single GPU, and a frontier training run uses tens of thousands of them.
02 / The Atomic Unit

Tokens: the units of thought

LLMs don't see characters and they don't see words. They see tokens, which are integer IDs into a fixed vocabulary, learned from data. Every interaction with an LLM starts and ends with tokenization.

When you type a prompt, the first thing that happens, before any neural network gets involved, is that your text is broken into tokens. A token might be a whole word ("the"), a piece of a word ("ing"), a single character, or even a single byte. The model's vocabulary is the fixed set of tokens it knows; everything outside that set has to be expressed as a combination of tokens that are inside it.

Byte Pair Encoding, in one breath

The dominant algorithm is Byte Pair Encoding (BPE), originally a 1994 text compression technique. The training procedure is almost embarrassingly simple:

  1. Start with a base vocabulary of every individual byte (256 tokens).
  2. In your training corpus, find the most frequent pair of adjacent tokens.
  3. Add that pair as a new token to the vocabulary.
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until you hit your target vocabulary size.

The result: extremely common words like the, and, of get their own tokens. Common suffixes like -ing and -ed become tokens. Common prefixes in code like def or import become tokens. Rare or invented words get split into multiple tokens. Foreign script characters and emojis are encoded as multi byte sequences.

The tokenizer arms race

Vocabulary size matters. A bigger vocabulary means fewer tokens per text, which means faster inference, cheaper API calls, and more content per context window. It also means more parameters in the embedding matrix.

Special tokens

Every modern LLM has reserved tokens that aren't part of the natural language vocabulary: <BOS> (beginning of sequence), <EOS> (end), <PAD> (padding), and chat template tokens like <|im_start|>, <|endoftext|>, or Claude's <thinking> tags. These act as structural punctuation that the model learns to respect.

03 / The Architecture

The Transformer, dissected

In 2017, a team at Google published "Attention is All You Need." Almost every major LLM in 2026, including GPT-4, Claude, Llama, Gemini, DeepSeek, and Mistral, is a direct architectural descendant of that paper.

The original Transformer paper had an encoder and a decoder, designed for translation. Modern chat models are decoder only. A single tower of identical blocks, stacked 32 to 120 layers deep, with one job: given the tokens so far, predict the next one.

The decoder tower

Click any layer to see what happens there. The flow is bottom to top: integers become vectors, vectors get mixed and refined through dozens of attention+FFN passes, and the final hidden state of the last token becomes a probability distribution over the vocabulary.

For Llama 3.1 70B: 80 layers, 8192 hidden dim, 64 query heads, 8 KV heads (GQA), 28672 FFN dim, 128256 vocab.

A single decoder block contains five components, applied in this order to every token's vector:

  1. Layer Normalization (modern variants use RMSNorm). Keeps the numbers in a sane range so gradients don't explode or vanish.
  2. Multi Head Self Attention with a causal mask. The token "looks at" all earlier tokens and gathers information from them.
  3. Residual connection. The input to the layer is added back to the output. This is what allows training networks 100 layers deep.
  4. Feed Forward Network (FFN). Usually two linear layers with a non linearity (SwiGLU, GeLU) between them. The hidden width is typically four times the model dimension.
  5. Another residual connection.

Stack this block 32 to 120 times. Add an embedding lookup at the bottom and a linear projection to vocabulary at the top. That's the entire architecture.

Why decoder only won

The 2017 paper used both an encoder (for the input) and a decoder (for the output). BERT style models kept just the encoder. T5 and BART kept both. Today, almost every chat focused model is decoder only. Three reasons won the architecture war:

  • Simplicity. One tower of identical blocks is easier to scale, parallelize, and serve than two interacting towers.
  • Unification. The prompt and the response are just tokens in the same stream. Prefill and decode use the same machinery.
  • Empirical scaling. Decoder only models showed the cleanest power law improvements as you added parameters and data.
04 / The Beating Heart

Self attention, line by line

Strip away the framework code and the fancy diagrams, and self attention is three matrix multiplications and one softmax. Get this single equation in your bones and the rest of the architecture is just plumbing.

For an input matrix X ∈ ℝⁿˣᵈ (n tokens, each as a d dimensional vector), the model computes three projections using learned weight matrices:

Step 1 · Learned linear projections Q = X · WQ    K = X · WK    V = X · WV

The three projection matrices W^Q, W^K, W^V each have shape d × d_k. Then attention is computed as:

Step 2 · The attention equation Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q · Kᵀ / √dk ) · V

That's the entire equation that powers ChatGPT. Let's walk through every symbol.

The intuition: a soft dictionary lookup

Imagine each token throws up a hand and asks a question. That's its query vector Q. Every token (including itself) holds up a label. That's its key vector K. And every token also carries a payload of information, its value vector V.

  • The dot product Q · Kᵀ measures how well each query matches each key. High score means "this earlier token is relevant to me."
  • The scaling factor √dk prevents the dot products from growing too large as d_k grows. Without it, softmax would saturate and gradients would die.
  • The softmax turns the raw scores into a probability distribution over earlier tokens. They sum to 1.
  • Finally, the model takes a weighted average of the value vectors V using those probabilities as weights. The result is a new vector that "blends in" the most relevant earlier information.
"It's a soft, differentiable dictionary lookup where every token gets to query every other token."

Multi head attention

One attention computation gives the model one "way of looking" at the sequence. But language is multi faceted. You might want one head tracking syntax, another tracking coreference, another tracking semantic similarity. The solution is multi head attention: run the whole attention computation h times in parallel, each on a smaller slice of the hidden dimension (size d/h), then concatenate the results.

Llama 3 70B uses 64 query heads. GPT-4 reportedly uses 128. Each head is computationally cheap because it operates on smaller vectors, but together they let the model attend to many different patterns simultaneously.

The complexity wall

The score matrix QKᵀ has shape n×n where n is the sequence length. So self attention is O(n²) in both time and memory. Doubling the context length quadruples the cost. This is why context windows used to top out at 2K to 4K tokens, and why every modern efficiency innovation (FlashAttention, GQA, sliding windows, Mamba) is in some way an attack on this quadratic wall.

05 / Order Matters

Positional encoding: telling the model where things are

Self attention is permutation invariant. If you scrambled the order of the tokens, the attention computation would produce the exact same set of output vectors (just shuffled). The model would have no way to distinguish "dog bites man" from "man bites dog."

The fix is to inject position information into the token vectors before they enter the transformer stack. There are two dominant approaches.

Sinusoidal (the original, 2017)

Vaswani et al. proposed adding fixed sin/cos waves of geometrically increasing wavelengths directly to the token embeddings. Position 1 gets one fingerprint of waves; position 2 gets a slightly different one. The model learns to read these fingerprints.

RoPE, Rotary Position Embeddings (the modern winner)

Introduced by Su et al. in 2021, RoPE takes a more elegant approach: rotate the Q and K vectors in 2D sub spaces by an angle proportional to their absolute position. The mathematical beauty is that when you take the dot product Q · K, the absolute positions cancel out, leaving only a function of the relative distance between two tokens.

RoPE has effectively become the default. It's used in Llama 2/3, Mistral, PaLM, CodeGen, Mixtral, DeepSeek, Gemma, and most production models. It's parameter free, plays nicely with FlashAttention kernels, and extrapolates better to long contexts than sinusoidal encoding.

The Transformer's elegance is that information flows through a stack of identical blocks. Each one selectively gathers context from earlier tokens via attention, then locally transforms each token via the feed forward network.

06 / The Pipeline

What happens when you press Enter

You type a prompt, click send, and a few seconds later text streams back. Inside, the model runs in two distinct phases. One is compute bound. The other is memory bound. A single optimization called the KV cache is what makes the whole thing economically viable.

Let's trace exactly what happens when you submit the prompt "Explain entropy:" to an LLM.

  1. Tokenization. Your text becomes a sequence of integers, say [15837, 47148, 25] for those three tokens.
  2. Embedding lookup. Each integer indexes a row of the embedding matrix, producing a sequence of d dimensional vectors.
  3. Prefill phase (compute bound). The model processes the entire prompt in parallel through every transformer layer. For each layer, Q/K/V are computed for every token, attention is run, FFN runs, and crucially the K and V tensors for every token are written to a structure in GPU memory called the KV cache.
  4. Decode phase (memory bound). Now the model generates one token at a time. For each new token: compute Q, K, V for just the new token; append K and V to the cache; compute attention using the new Q against the cached K and V; run FFN; project to vocabulary logits; sample the next token.
  5. Output projection. The final hidden state of the last token is projected through the unembedding matrix to produce a vector of logits, one number per vocabulary entry. Softmax converts these to probabilities.
  6. Sampling. The next token is sampled from this distribution. We'll cover the strategies in the next section.
  7. Loop. Feed the new token back as input. Repeat until you hit an EOS token or the max tokens limit.

How the model chooses each token: sampling

At the end of every decode step, the model produces a vector of logits, one number per vocabulary entry. Softmax converts these into a probability distribution. Then a sampling strategy chooses one. The choice matters enormously. It is the difference between deterministic and creative, between repetitive and surprising.

08 / The Modern Toolkit

Variants that changed everything

The 2017 Transformer is the trunk. Everything since has been pruning, grafting, and engineering. Four innovations matter more than any others for understanding the LLMs you use today.

Mixture of Experts (MoE)

The most consequential architectural change since the original Transformer. A standard ("dense") transformer layer has one feed forward network that every token passes through. A Mixture of Experts layer has N expert FFNs and a small router network that decides which top-k experts each token should activate.

The result is a model with hundreds of billions of total parameters, but only tens of billions are active for any given token. You get the capability of a giant model with the per token inference cost of a much smaller one.

GPT-4 (leaked estimate) ~1,760 B ~280 B 16 / 2 2023

The catch: you still need VRAM to hold all the parameters, because you don't know in advance which experts a given token will route to. MoE saves compute but not memory.

Grouped Query Attention (GQA)

Standard multi head attention gives each query head its own K and V heads. With 64 query heads, that's 64 separate KV pairs per layer, and the KV cache is huge. The opposite extreme, Multi Query Attention (MQA), has all query heads share one KV head: much smaller cache, but quality suffers.

Grouped Query Attention (Ainslie et al., 2023) is the Goldilocks compromise. G groups of query heads, each group sharing one KV head. Llama 3 70B uses 64 query heads and 8 KV heads, an 8x KV cache reduction with near identical quality to full multi head attention. GQA is now the default in Llama, Mistral, Mixtral, Gemma, DeepSeek, and most production models.

FlashAttention

The attention computation produces an n×n score matrix. For n equals 100,000 tokens, that's a 10 billion entry matrix that doesn't fit anywhere reasonable. FlashAttention (Dao et al., NeurIPS 2022) is an IO aware algorithm that computes attention exactly, but never materializes the full score matrix. It tiles the computation into chunks that fit in fast on chip SRAM, doing the softmax incrementally with running statistics.

The numbers from the original paper, measured on 8x A100 GPUs: 3x faster GPT-2 training at sequence length 1K versus the standard HuggingFace implementation. Up to 20x more memory efficient. Enables sequence lengths of 16K to 64K that simply weren't tractable before.

State Space Models, the post Transformer threat

The Transformer's O(n²) attention is its Achilles' heel. State Space Models, and specifically Mamba (Gu and Dao, 2023, COLM 2024 Outstanding Paper), take a different approach: compress all sequence history into a fixed size hidden state that's updated as the sequence is processed. The result is O(n) instead of O(n²).

The Mamba paper claims that "Mamba-3B outperforms Transformers of the same size and matches Transformers twice its size." Hybrid architectures like Jamba interleave Mamba and attention layers, letting Mamba carry the long range gist cheaply while attention does precise lookups.

What modern Llamas actually use

The Llama 3.1 architecture combines: decoder only Transformer, RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, Grouped Query Attention, FlashAttention-2 kernels. None of those acronyms existed in the original 2017 paper. The trunk is the same. The foliage is entirely new.

09 / The Education

How models learn

Pre-training is the long, expensive phase that creates a "base model", a text completion engine with no manners. Fine-tuning and RLHF are what turn it into a helpful assistant.

Pre-training: next token prediction at scale

The model is shown a sliding window of text from a vast corpus: web pages, books, code, papers, transcripts. At every position, it tries to predict the next token. The loss function is cross entropy between its predicted distribution and the actual next token. Gradients flow back, the AdamW optimizer updates every parameter slightly, and the process repeats for trillions of tokens.

For Llama 3.1 405B, Meta reports the training run consumed over 16,000 H100 GPUs for months, totaling roughly 30.8 million H100 hours. The corpus was over 15 trillion tokens, roughly the entire English language internet plus a great deal more.

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)

After pre-training, the base model is a text completion engine. Given "The capital of France is", it'll happily output "Paris." Given "Help me write an email to my landlord", it might just continue with another similar instruction instead of writing the email. SFT teaches it to follow instructions by training on a curated dataset of (prompt, ideal response) pairs, but only computing loss on the response tokens, not the prompt.

RLHF · Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

Even an instruction tuned model can be unhelpful, untruthful, or unsafe. The final polish comes from RLHF:

  1. Generate multiple responses to the same prompt from the SFT model.
  2. Have human annotators rank them (or pick the best one).
  3. Train a separate reward model to predict which response a human would prefer.
  4. Fine-tune the original LLM with reinforcement learning (PPO or DPO) to maximize the reward model's score, with a KL divergence penalty keeping it close to the original.

Almost every major chat model (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama 3 Instruct) went through some version of RLHF. Anthropic's variant, Constitutional AI (RLAIF), has the model critique its own outputs against a written list of principles and uses those critiques as the feedback signal, scaling beyond what human labelers can produce.

The compute imbalance

Training is a one time cost: spend $50M, get a frontier model. But after that, every API call runs the model again. The operational lifecycle of a typical deployed LLM has inference consuming more than 90% of the total energy. Over a model's life, inference dwarfs training.

The training cluster for a single frontier model is a building sized supercomputer. Tens of thousands of GPUs, exotic networking, dedicated substations for power, and chilled water loops for cooling. Inference happens at this scale too, but spread across the world, 24/7.
10 / Silicon

Why GPUs, not CPUs

A modern CPU has a few dozen cores optimized for branchy, sequential, general purpose work. A modern GPU has tens of thousands of simple arithmetic units organized for one thing: matrix multiplication. That is not a coincidence. That is the entire game.

Every transformer layer is dominated by large matrix multiplies (GEMM operations). Attention is (n × d) × (d × n). The feed forward network is (n × d) × (d × 4d). These map perfectly onto GPU tensor cores, specialized matrix math units that can multiply two 4×4 matrices in a single clock cycle.

An H100 has 528 tensor cores, plus 16,896 standard CUDA cores, all running in parallel. Where a CPU might do 100 GFLOPS of dense FP32 matrix math, an H100 does 67 TFLOPS in FP32 and 989 TFLOPS in BF16. That's a 700x ratio for the kind of arithmetic LLMs actually need.

For matrix multiplication, the GPU wins by roughly 700x. That single ratio is the entire economic case for AI hardware. CPUs were designed for sequential, branchy logic: web servers, databases, operating systems. GPUs were designed for graphics, which turned out to be the same math as deep learning. A happy accident that built a trillion dollar industry.

The real bottleneck: memory bandwidth

Here is the counterintuitive truth about modern LLM inference: most of the time, the GPU isn't doing arithmetic. It's waiting for data to arrive from memory.

During the decode phase, every new token requires reading the entire model's weights from HBM (high bandwidth memory) just once. If your model is 140 GB and your HBM bandwidth is 3.35 TB/s, the absolute theoretical floor on decode speed is 3,350 ÷ 140 ≈ 24 tokens/sec per request. And that's ignoring the KV cache and all overhead.

This is why "FLOPS" alone is a misleading way to compare AI chips. The real spec sheet has three lines: memory capacity (how big a model fits), memory bandwidth (how fast can you stream weights), and compute (how fast can you crunch).

11 / The Memory Math

Why a 70B model won't fit on your gaming GPU

Memory math for LLMs is the most useful piece of arithmetic an engineer can know. The rule is simple:

VRAM rule of thumb VRAM (GB) ≈ params (billions) × bytes_per_param × overhead

Different numerical formats use different numbers of bytes per parameter. FP32 uses 4 bytes, FP16 and BF16 use 2 bytes, FP8 uses 1 byte, INT4 uses 0.5 bytes. Quantization is the practice of storing weights in lower precision formats, usually with surprisingly little quality loss. Plus you need about 20% overhead for the KV cache, activations, and the framework itself.

12 / Scaling Out

When one GPU isn't enough

A 70B model in FP16 doesn't fit on a single H100. A frontier model needs thousands of GPUs working together. The art of splitting a model across hardware has its own vocabulary, and its own physics.

Four flavors of parallelism

  • Data parallelism. Replicate the entire model on every GPU. Each GPU processes a different slice of the batch. Memory cost is high, every GPU holds the full model.
  • Tensor parallelism. Split each individual matrix multiplication across multiple GPUs. Best done within a single server over NVLink (about 900 GB/s per H100).
  • Pipeline parallelism. Assign different layers to different GPUs in an assembly line. Communication is light, but there are "bubbles" of idle time.
  • Expert parallelism. For MoE models: put different experts on different GPUs. Network heavy.

GPT-4 reportedly trained with 8-way tensor times 15-way pipeline parallelism, and inference uses 8-way tensor times 16-way pipeline on 128 GPU clusters.

The networking equation

Within a server: NVLink connects 8 GPUs at 900 GB/s each (H100) or 1.8 TB/s (B200). Between servers: InfiniBand NDR at 400 Gb/s per port, or XDR at 800 Gb/s. NVIDIA's GB200 NVL72 rack puts 72 Blackwell GPUs in a single NVLink domain, turning a whole rack into what looks like one giant GPU. Power draw is roughly 120 kW per rack, liquid cooled.

How modern inference got cheap

The 100x price drop in output tokens between GPT-4 launch and DeepSeek-V3 didn't come from a single breakthrough. It came from a stack of optimizations, each compounding on the others:

  • KV cache. The foundation. Every modern serving stack assumes its presence.
  • Quantization. Store weights in INT8, INT4, or FP4. Modern algorithms lose less than 1% on most benchmarks. INT4 cuts memory 4x versus FP16.
  • FlashAttention 2/3. Fuses attention operations into a single GPU kernel that minimizes HBM traffic.
  • PagedAttention (vLLM). OS style paging for the KV cache. Pushes utilization from 20 to 40% up to roughly 96%.
  • Continuous batching. Re-batch requests at every decode step. New requests don't wait for old ones to finish.
  • Speculative decoding. A small "draft" model proposes 4 to 8 candidate tokens. The big "target" model verifies them all in a single parallel forward pass. 2 to 3x speedup with identical output distribution.
  • Prefill-decode disaggregation. Route compute-bound prefill to one pool of GPUs and memory-bound decode to a separate pool.
13 / The Frontier

What's new in 2025 to 2026

Long context (1M+ tokens)

Gemini 1.5 Pro demonstrated greater than 99% needle in a haystack recall up to 1 million tokens (and 10 million in research). Claude and GPT-4 Turbo support 200K and 128K respectively. Long context is enabled by efficient attention variants, careful position encoding extrapolation, and massive engineering investment in serving infrastructure. Cost scales roughly linearly with input length. A 1M token prompt is about 8x more expensive than 128K.

Reasoning models · a new scaling axis

OpenAI's o1 (Sep 2024), o3, DeepSeek-R1, and Claude's "extended thinking" mode introduced a different way to scale: instead of just training bigger models, spend more compute at inference time. These models are trained with reinforcement learning to generate long internal chains of thought before producing a final answer.

The numbers are striking. On hard math competition problems (AIME), o1 went from about 14% accuracy (GPT-4o) to about 83%. The trade-off: each reasoning response can use 10,000 to 100,000 internal "thinking" tokens, multiplying inference cost. But for problems where correctness matters more than latency, this is a fair trade.

MoE at scale

DeepSeek-V3's fine-grained MoE (256 routed experts, top-8 selection) combined with auxiliary-loss-free load balancing, multi-token prediction, and FP8 training reportedly cost only about $5.6M for the final training run, versus the $60M+ of dense competitors. (Caveat: this figure excludes R&D, failed runs, and human labor.)

Distillation

Large teacher models generate synthetic training data for much smaller student models. GPT-4o-mini, Claude Haiku, Llama-3.2-3B and Gemma 3 all use distillation extensively. The result: 10 to 100x cheaper inference with most of the capability intact for routine tasks.

Every algorithmic innovation eventually has to map onto silicon. The 100x drop in inference cost is a story of co-design between model architectures, software kernels, and hardware features.
14 / The Economics

The great deflation

Three years ago, generating a million tokens cost $60. Today it costs as little as 28 cents. Few technologies in history have deflated this quickly. The charts below tell the story.

The curve has three drivers, each one we've covered separately:

  • Algorithmic efficiency. Mixture of Experts decoupled total parameters from per token compute. Grouped Query Attention shrunk KV caches. FlashAttention slashed memory traffic.
  • Hardware progression. A100 → H100 → H200 → B200 each delivered roughly 2 to 3x more useful work per dollar per second.
  • Software engineering. vLLM style continuous batching pushed GPU utilization from about 40% to over 90%. Speculative decoding multiplied effective throughput. Quantization halved memory needs again.

The cost of training a frontier model is moving in the opposite direction. GPT-3 cost about $5M in 2020. GPT-4 cost an estimated $60M to $100M in 2023. The next generation of frontier models is widely reported to cost in the $500M to $1B range when you include failed runs, R&D, and labor. Training is the moat. Inference is the commodity.

Energy per query

How much electricity does a single ChatGPT query use? Per Epoch AI's February 2025 analysis, a typical GPT-4o query consumes about 0.3 watt-hours. That is about ten times less than the older 3 Wh estimate, because both models and hardware have become more efficient. Sam Altman cited a corroborating 0.34 Wh per query in his June 2025 blog post.

For context, that's roughly what an oven uses in a single second, or a high efficiency LED bulb uses in about two minutes. The figure goes up dramatically for long context queries. Epoch AI estimates that a 100K token input can exceed 40 Wh per query, and reasoning models with extended thinking traces can be 10x higher again.

At population scale, these small numbers add up. Hundreds of millions of daily ChatGPT queries put real strain on power grids. This is why every major AI company is now in the business of negotiating power contracts and, in some cases, commissioning new nuclear capacity.

15 / The Whole Picture

A single query, end to end

Let's put every concept together. You type "Write a haiku about autumn" into a chat interface. Here is the full life of that query, from keystroke to response:

  1. HTTP request. Your text travels to the provider's API gateway. The router determines which model server pool can handle it.
  2. Tokenization. The provider's tokenizer converts your text into integer IDs. "Write a haiku about autumn" becomes about 6 tokens.
  3. Prefix cache lookup. Modern serving stacks check if any KV cache entries already exist for this prompt's prefix.
  4. Prefill. The full prompt is processed in one parallel forward pass through every transformer layer. K and V tensors are written to GPU HBM. Time: about 50 to 200 ms on an H100 for short prompts.
  5. Decode loop. For each output token: compute attention against the cached K/V, run the FFN (or in MoE, dispatch to the right experts), produce logits, sample, write the new token's K/V to the cache, send the token to the client over a streaming connection.
  6. You see tokens appear, one at a time, at 30 to 150 per second.
  7. End of sequence. The model samples an EOS token, signaling the response is complete. The KV cache is held in memory in case you reply, then eventually evicted.
  8. Billing. The provider counts your input tokens (cheaper) and output tokens (more expensive, because each one required a sequential decode step), and your account is debited.

Multiply that pipeline by hundreds of millions of users sending billions of queries per day, running on hundreds of thousands of GPUs spread across data centers on three continents, drawing tens of megawatts of grid power continuously. That is the operational reality of modern LLMs.

"The model is just a function. The miracle is the infrastructure that lets you call it."
16 / The Fine Print

What we actually know, and what we don't

Every concrete number in this guide comes with footnotes. Closed model architectures are estimates. Training cost figures involve heroic assumptions. Energy numbers are population averages.

On GPT-4's architecture

The "1.76 trillion parameters / 16 experts" figures for GPT-4 come from SemiAnalysis reporting in mid 2023 and have never been confirmed by OpenAI. Treat them as informed estimates, not facts.

On training costs

Companies rarely publish actual training run costs. Figures like "$60M for GPT-4" combine GPU hour estimates with publicly known cluster sizes and rental rates. They typically exclude R&D salaries, failed runs, data acquisition, and post training human labor, which often double or triple the all in number.

On the DeepSeek $5.6M figure

The widely quoted "$5.6 million for DeepSeek-V3" reflects only the final, successful training run. It excludes the R&D iterations, failed experiments, data preparation, and the human labor stack, making direct comparison with other labs' all in figures unfair.

On API pricing

Quoted prices were accurate at time of writing but change frequently. Always check live pricing pages for current rates.

On reasoning models

"Reasoning model" is partly a marketing label. Models like o1, o3, DeepSeek-R1, and Claude with extended thinking are still Transformers underneath. What changed is the training objective (heavy RL on chain of thought) and the inference time behavior (much longer outputs before the final answer). The architecture class hasn't changed.

On energy numbers

The "0.3 Wh per query" figure (Epoch AI, 2025) is a population average for typical short ChatGPT interactions. Long context queries, multimodal queries, and reasoning model queries can be 10 to 100x higher.
